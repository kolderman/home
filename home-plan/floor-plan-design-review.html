Floor plan design review

The bones are good: a true single-level plan with the primary suite and the kids' wing at opposite ends, a scullery pantry, a lounge that gives the bedrooms their own living space, and porches on three sides. The problems are in the details: bedroom 3 is too shallow, the kitchen has no range and the island is pinched against the fridge wall, there is no mudroom or entry, and the plan has no mechanical room. All of these are fixable before the next draft.

How this was reviewed

I measured draft G against the standards architects and kitchen designers use (NKBA kitchen planning guidelines, the residential code minimums for rooms and stairs, universal design practice) and against the patterns that show up again and again in well-regarded single-level plans: a defined entry, a mudroom between the garage and the house, light on two sides of every main room, a scullery behind the kitchen, the primary suite separated from the other bedrooms, and a second living space for the kids' side. The reference points are Christopher Alexander's A Pattern Language, Sarah Susanka's The Not So Big House, and the best-selling farmhouse and ranch plans from Southern Living and similar plan houses, which share most of those moves. Dimensions are from the draft G generator, rounded.

What is working

Zoning
The plan reads as three zones: the primary suite and office on the right, the great room in the middle, and the kids' or guest wing on the left behind the lounge. That is the layout most well-rated split-bedroom plans use. Each bedroom has its own bath, which is a real upgrade for guests and for resale.
The lounge
A second living room on the bedroom side is what Susanka calls an "away room." It lets the kids or guests have the TV on while the great room is quiet. Placing it between the great room and the bedrooms also puts a buffer between the two.
Scullery pantry
A walk-in pantry with a sink and counter behind the kitchen is one of the most requested features in new custom homes. The grocery hatch from the garage is a clever touch.
Light on two sides
The living room gets light from the front windows and the back porch wall. Alexander argues that rooms lit from two sides are the ones people gravitate to, and the main room has it.
Outdoor rooms
The back porch runs the full width of the living room and connects to the outdoor kitchen and dining. The side porch off the primary gives the owners a private outdoor space. That is three distinct outdoor rooms, which is more than most plans manage.
Single level
Everything you need day to day is on one floor. With 36-inch doors, a step-free garage entry and a curbless primary shower, this plan would carry you through any stage of life.

Issues to fix before the next draft

Ordered by how much they matter. The first four change how the house lives; the rest are refinements.

Bedroom 3 is too shallowAfter the 4-foot vestibule it is 17'6" wide by only 9'4" deep. A queen bed is 6'8" long, which leaves 2'8" to walk past it. Most plans use 12 by 12 feet as the floor for a secondary bedroom. Your wife already said the bedroom sizing was off and she would redraw the wing; aim for at least 12 feet of depth, and let bath 3 and the closet shrink to get there.
No range or cooktopThe kitchen shows a sink, two dishwashers and a fridge but nothing to cook on. A range on the front wall would compete with the sink under the window; the natural spot is the back wall near the dining room, which also puts the vent hood on an exterior wall. Decide this now because it drives the hood, gas line and the whole work triangle.
Island is too close to the fridge wallThe island's right edge is about 28 inches from the fridge and counter run. NKBA calls for 42 inches minimum, 48 if two people cook, and the fridge door needs room to swing. Either narrow the island to 4 feet or move it left; with 16 feet of kitchen width you can have a 4 by 10 foot island and 48 inches on both sides.
No mudroom or drop zoneThe garage door opens straight into the hall by the stairs. In nearly every well-rated plan there is a mudroom between the garage and the house with a bench, hooks and a closet. The original sketch had lockers on the garage's back wall. A 6 by 8 foot mudroom carved from the garage corner, or built-ins along the hall wall where the bench already is, would do it.
No mechanical roomA 4,400 sf house needs two or three HVAC air handlers, a water heater or two, a water softener, the electrical panels and, with a server room, a structured wiring panel. Nothing on the plan holds them. Attic units are possible, but a 6 by 8 foot mechanical room off the garage or the laundry is cheaper to service and quieter. Plan it now, before the roof shape is set.
Front door opens straight into the living roomThere is no foyer, no coat closet and no place to set things down. Alexander calls this the "entrance transition": a short, defined threshold makes the big room feel bigger. Even a 6-foot-deep entry with a closet, marked by a lower ceiling or a change in floor, would fix it. The planter is a nice idea for that spot but it sits in the walking line from the door to the kitchen.
Stairs are oversizedThe stair box is 10'6" by 14'6". With 10-foot ceilings you need about 18 risers, which a U-shaped stair handles in roughly 7 feet wide by 11 feet long, landing included. Shrinking it gives the pantry and the under-stair storage back about 3 feet. Also, the stair lands in the hall by the garage door, which is the right place for a bonus room: it keeps the upstairs traffic out of the main rooms.
Laundry shares a wall with the primary bedroomThe washer and dryer sit against the bedroom's foot wall. Insulate that wall, put the machines on the far side of the room, or swap the laundry and trash bump-in so the quieter space is against the bedroom.
Grill against the office wallThe outdoor kitchen's grill sits on the back run of the U, against the house. Gas grills need clearance from combustible siding, and smoke will go in the office windows. Put the grill on the open leg of the U facing the yard, with the sink and counters against the house.
Server room locationA rack throws heat and fan noise. It is on the bedroom hall with a wall shared with bedroom 3's TV wall. Keep it, but give it a dedicated mini-split or a supply and return, a solid-core door, and insulation in the walls. An alternative is a closet off the office, where the noise matters less and the structured wiring is likely to land anyway.

Things to decide that the sketch cannot

Orientation
Which way is north decides everything about the porches. The back porch and pond side want to face north or east so the living room gets soft light and the porch stays usable in the afternoon. If the back faces west, the 14-foot porch depth is doing real work and the primary's side porch will be hot.
Ceilings
Use height to mark the rooms. Ten feet in the bedrooms and office, twelve or a vault in the living room, and a dropped ceiling of nine feet or so at the entry and in the pantry. Susanka's point is that the tall room feels taller when you pass through a low one to get there.
Roof
A 113-foot-long single-story house with a bonus room over the garage will have a complicated roof. The primary suite and office wing step back 10 feet from the main block, which helps. Ask the designer to study a simple gable over the main block with the wings as hips or lower gables, and to keep the bonus room's roof inside the garage's footprint.
Storage
There is no coat closet, no linen closet and no broom closet on the plan. Add a coat closet at the entry, a linen closet in the bedroom hall (it was there in draft F before we removed it) and a utility closet near the laundry.
Upstairs
If the bonus room will ever be a bedroom, it needs an egress window and a bath, which option 1 has. Rough in the plumbing even if you finish it later.
Budget
At roughly 4,400 heated square feet plus 770 sf of bonus space, a 900 sf garage and 1,050 sf of porches, this is a large custom house. Square footage, not finish level, is the biggest lever on cost. Bedroom 2, the office and bath 2 are the three rooms that could lose area without losing anything.

Suggested checklist for the next draft

  1. 1Redraw the bedroom wing with bedroom 3 at least 12 feet deep. Take the depth from bath 3 and the closet, or the width from bedroom 2.
  2. 2Place the range and hood, and narrow or shift the island to get 42 inches on every side.
  3. 3Add a mudroom or built-in drop zone between the garage door and the hall.
  4. 4Add a mechanical room, a coat closet at the entry and a linen closet in the bedroom hall.
  5. 5Tighten the stair to about 7 by 11 feet and give the space back to the pantry.
  6. 6Mark the entry with a dropped ceiling or a floor change, and move the planter out of the path.
  7. 7Move the grill to the open side of the outdoor kitchen.
  8. 8Settle north, and then decide ceiling heights and porch depths with the sun in mind.
